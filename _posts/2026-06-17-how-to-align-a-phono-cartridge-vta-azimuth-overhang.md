Cartridge alignment separates casual listeners from vinyl enthusiasts who hear every detail in their records. Three angles control how the stylus contacts the groove, and getting all three right delivers the most accurate playback your turntable is capable of. Most collectors never optimize these settings, leaving performance on the table.

This guide walks you through each alignment parameter: overhang (forward-back positioning), azimuth (left-right tilt), and VTA (vertical angle). You'll learn why each matters, how to measure it, and how to adjust with tools you can buy today.

## Understanding overhang and tracking accuracy

Overhang describes how far the stylus extends beyond the tonearm pivot point. This distance affects which part of the groove the stylus contacts. If overhang is too short, the stylus drags across the outer groove wall. Too long, and it drags the inner wall. Neither condition is desirable - both introduce distortion and accelerate groove wear.

Manufacturers specify correct overhang in millimeters, typically 15 to 18mm depending on tonearm design. Measure from the tonearm's pivot point (where it swings) to the stylus tip. Most adjustable headshells have a screw that slides the cartridge forward or backward to hit this target.

Correct overhang also ensures the stylus traces the groove's center, where the stereo information is strongest. This is why a protractor tool is invaluable. The [Audio-Technica Stylus Alignment Tool (Cartridge Protractor)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FWKWXHKT?tag=analogrevivalist-20) lets you visually confirm overhang by placing it on a record. Align the cartridge's reference marks to the protractor lines, then tighten the headshell mounting screws. For detailed manufacturer specifications on cartridge geometry, consult [Ortofon's cartridge specification guides](https://www.ortofon.com/en-us/products), which document optimal alignment parameters for their product range.

## Azimuth adjustment and stereo separation

Azimuth is the left-right tilt of the cartridge. Imagine the stylus should sit perfectly vertical when viewed head-on. If it tilts left or right, contact with the groove becomes uneven, and the stylus presses harder on one channel than the other.

The result: stereo imaging collapses. You lose the precise left-right positioning that makes a great recording feel three-dimensional. On properly aligned cartridges, singers sit center stage, instruments appear left and right, and the soundstage feels wide and open. Misaligned azimuth makes everything collapse to mono or shift center.

Most headshells have mounting points that allow 3-5 degrees of tilt adjustment. Some cartridges ship with alignment marks etched on the body - align these marks square to your turntable's line, using a straightedge or the alignment protractor. If your cartridge lacks marks, tilt the headshell until the body appears vertical.

Fine-tuning azimuth requires listening. Play a stereo recording with clear left and right elements - a jazz recording with drums on one side and bass on the other works well. Adjust the headshell tilt in tiny increments and listen for the image to center. When it does, tighten the mounting screws. This takes patience, but the payoff is instant - stereo separation jumps dramatically.

## VTA: vertical tracking angle and tonal balance

VTA describes the angle the tonearm makes relative to the record surface. Manufacturers design tonearms to sit at a specific VTA, typically 92 degrees measured at the pivot point. This angle ensures the stylus contacts the groove at the correct perpendicularity.

Incorrect VTA colors the sound. Too shallow (arm tilted back), and you get a duller, darker character with reduced treble. Too steep (arm tilted forward), and the sound becomes bright and thin. The cartridge manufacturer spent time tuning the stylus shape to work at their specified angle - respecting that angle gives you the intended character.

### VTA adjustment on specific tonearm types

Different tonearm designs approach VTA adjustment in very different ways. Understanding your specific arm type avoids frustration and wasted adjustment attempts.

**Rega tonearms** (RB110, RB220, RB330, and similar) are fixed-VTA designs. Rega explicitly engineered their arms to sit at the correct VTA for standard 1.5mm-thick LPs, and they offer no height adjustment on the main pivot. Some third-party shims are available that go between the arm base and the armboard to raise or lower the pivot - these work but require removing the arm. Rega's position is that the factory angle is correct, and adjustments are unnecessary for most records. In practice, thicker audiophile pressings (180-gram vinyl) sit slightly higher and can cause Rega arms to run slightly nose-down. If you play many 180-gram records, a 1mm shim under the arm base corrects this.

**SME tonearms** (Series III, Series IV, and V) offer genuine VTA adjustment via a setscrew at the arm base. Loosen the setscrew, slide the arm up or down to change the pivot height, and retighten. SME arms include a scale marked in millimeters so you can return to a known position if you experiment. For SME Series III with a pivot-to-spindle distance of 215.4mm, the manufacturer recommends starting with the armtube parallel to the record surface. Measure from the headshell bottom to the LP surface at the spindle end - it should match the measurement at the pivot end within 1mm.

**Thorens tonearms** (TP16, TP19, TP29, and TD variants) vary by model but most include some form of VTA adjustment via a clamping ring near the bearing housing. On the TP16 MkIV, turn the locking ring counterclockwise to release the arm tube, slide it up or down, then retighten. The TP29 uses a similar system. Thorens documentation typically specifies factory VTA by arm height in millimeters above the plinth surface - check your specific manual before adjusting.

Most vintage tonearms don't allow VTA adjustment - they're fixed at the factory setting. If your tonearm is one of these, you're done: the factory got it right for standard LPs. For technical reference on VTA and tracking geometry, [Vinyl Engine maintains detailed discussions](https://www.vinylengine.com/) of these parameters documented by experienced technicians.

VTA affects tracking force slightly, so after a major adjustment, recheck tracking force with your gauge. A good [Ortofon Tracking Force Gauge Digital](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00D8RTQMA?tag=analogrevivalist-20) ensures you're within manufacturer spec after any VTA change.

## Cartridge body geometry and compliance matching

Before diving into fine alignment work, confirm that your cartridge and tonearm are a compatible pair. Mismatched compliance and effective mass produce resonance peaks that no amount of alignment fixes.

### Effective mass and cartridge compliance

Every tonearm has an effective mass - the mass the cartridge "sees" when it tries to track a groove. Higher effective mass tonearms (above 20 grams) suit low-compliance cartridges, meaning cartridges with stiff cantilever suspensions. Lower effective mass tonearms (below 10 grams) pair with high-compliance cartridges, meaning cartridges with flexible suspensions. The target is a resonant frequency between 8 and 12Hz - low enough to stay below audible frequencies, high enough to stay above warp wobble (0.5-4Hz).

Calculate your combination's resonant frequency using the standard formula: F = 1000 / (2 * pi * sqrt((M + m) * C)), where M is effective tonearm mass in grams, m is cartridge mass in grams, and C is dynamic compliance in mm/mN (micrometers per millinewton). Many vintage tonearms publish effective mass in their specifications. Cartridge compliance is listed in manufacturer documentation, measured at 100Hz or 10Hz (the 10Hz measurement is more common in vintage specs - divide by 1.6 to approximate the 100Hz value if you're comparing across eras).

### What happens when compliance is mismatched

Mismatched combinations produce a resonance peak that distorts records. An overly stiff cartridge in a low effective mass arm results in a resonant frequency above 12Hz, inside the audible range. You'll hear this as a rising peak in the upper bass - a slight boominess or thickness that sounds like the system has extra bass, but isn't musical. Conversely, a high-compliance cartridge in a heavy arm resonates below 8Hz, which amplifies warp wobble and causes tracking problems on any record that isn't perfectly flat.

Common vintage pairing problems: Shure V15 series (high compliance, 20-25 CU at 10Hz) paired with heavy vintage Japanese arms (18-22 grams effective mass) runs at the low end of the resonance window. Acceptable, but the cartridge prefers a lighter arm. Ortofon OM series (medium compliance, 20 CU) handles a wide range of arms and works well with most vintage setups. Audio-Technica AT3600 (high compliance, 40 CU) genuinely needs a lightweight arm under 12 grams to stay in spec.

Check this calculation once before you install any cartridge. It takes five minutes and saves hours of troubleshooting a system that simply isn't matched correctly.

## Step-by-step alignment procedure

Follow this sequence to align any cartridge:

1. **Set overhang first.** Place the alignment protractor on the turntable (no record required). Lower the tonearm onto the protractor and position the cartridge until the stylus aligns with the two protractor markings. Tighten the headshell mounting screws evenly so you don't shift the cartridge.

2. **Verify azimuth next.** Look head-on at the cartridge body - does it appear vertical or tilted? If tilted, loosen one mounting screw slightly and tighten the opposite screw until the cartridge sits vertical. Make micro-adjustments, about a quarter-turn at a time.

3. **Check tracking force.** Place the tracking force gauge on the turntable, lower the stylus, and adjust the counterweight until the gauge reads the manufacturer's specification (typically 2.0 to 2.5 grams). Recheck overhang - counterweight adjustment sometimes shifts the tonearm slightly.

4. **Test VTA if adjustable.** Using a protractor marked for VTA (some include both overhang and VTA reference lines), confirm your arm is at the correct angle. Adjust if needed, then recheck tracking force.

5. **Listen for 10 minutes.** Play a familiar stereo recording and notice: Does the soundstage feel wide? Do voices sound centered? Is the treble clear or harsh? Make small azimuth tweaks (one-eighth turn on mounting screws) if the image seems off-center.

6. **Let settings stabilize.** New cartridges take 12-24 hours to settle. Suspension compliance changes slightly as the cartridge acclimates to your tonearm's mass. Recheck all three parameters after 24 hours and make final micro-adjustments.

Don't obsess over tiny adjustments. Within 0.5 degrees of overhang and azimuth, the ear can't resolve the difference. What matters is consistency and respect for manufacturer specs.

## Troubleshooting and best practices

### Misalignment symptoms

If your setup sounds wrong, one of three parameters is likely off. Use these symptoms to diagnose:

- **Harsh treble or bright character**: Likely over-forward azimuth or steep VTA. Tilt the cartridge back (clockwise from the front) or reduce arm angle if VTA is adjustable.

- **Dull, dark, missing treble**: Under-forward azimuth or shallow VTA. Tilt the cartridge slightly forward (counter-clockwise) or increase arm angle.

- **Collapsed stereo imaging**: Almost always azimuth. Even a 1-degree tilt noticeably affects imaging. Check and correct azimuth as the first troubleshooting step.

- **Tracking force too light (skipping inner grooves)**: Overhang too far back. Move the cartridge forward in the headshell. Or tracking force is genuinely too light - increase with counterweight.

- **Record damage or groove wear**: Tracking force too heavy or overhang offset (wrong cartridge position). Check both immediately and correct.

- **Persistent hum in one channel**: Often misread as a cartridge or preamp problem, but can indicate an azimuth error severe enough to cause channel imbalance at the signal level. Confirm azimuth before replacing components.

- **Inner groove distortion only**: A common complaint where the last few minutes of an album side sound harsh or distorted while the outer grooves play cleanly. This is frequently an overhang error - the stylus diverges from the groove tangent at inner radii more severely when overhang is set incorrectly. Re-run overhang alignment before suspecting a worn stylus.

### Common mistakes to avoid

Many collectors skip alignment altogether, assuming the factory got it right. Sometimes they did - modern mass-produced turntables often leave the factory aligned. But older gear, used turntables, or any cartridge replacement needs proper setup.

Another mistake is conflating alignment with upgrade. Upgrading a cartridge without realigning wastes half the upgrade's value. Every time you install a different cartridge, recheck all three parameters.

Some people chase extreme VTA or azimuth adjustments looking for tonal coloration. Alignment exists to let the music through, not to color it. Get to spec and stop. Your job is setting up the equipment correctly, not using it as a tone control.

Finally, don't adjust while the motor is running or the stylus is down on a record. Always kill the motor and lift the arm clear before making changes. A slip can scratch records and damage the cartridge.

## FAQ

**Q: How often should I realign my cartridge?**

A: Once at installation, then every 6-12 months. Mechanical changes from wear, temperature variations, or arm component settling can shift alignment slightly. An annual check keeps everything within spec.

**Q: Can I use a smartphone level app to check VTA?**

A: Smartphone levels are accurate to about 1 degree, which is close enough for home use. Place the phone on your tonearm's body (not the headshell) and check the angle against a known-level surface. This is fine for rough confirmation, but a proper protractor is more precise.

**Q: What if my tonearm doesn't allow overhang adjustment?**

A: Fixed-headshell tonearms (like some Technics models) don't permit overhang adjustment. In this case, the manufacturer engineered the overhang into the arm design. You're locked in, but the factory specs are correct. Focus on azimuth and VTA (if adjustable).

**Q: Does a slight overhang error (off by 1-2mm) really matter?**

A: Measurably, yes - you lose some stereo separation and add slight distortion. Audibly, it depends on your ears and your equipment. Systematic measurements show 5-10% THD increase at 2mm overhang error. Most casual listeners won't hear it, but anyone listening for detail will.

**Q: Should I align before or after the stylus settles on a new cartridge?**

A: Align first, then let the cartridge settle for 12-24 hours before fine-tuning azimuth. The suspension compliance changes as the cartridge acclimates, slightly shifting effective tracking force. Do a rough alignment, let it sit, then make final adjustments.

## The long view

Proper alignment costs you an hour of work and $60-100 in tools. The return is better sound, longer record life, and the satisfaction of knowing your equipment is running as designed. It's not glamorous - nobody upgrades their turntable and brags about alignment adjustments. But alignment is where most of the improvement lives.

Many listeners spend thousands on cartridges and tone arms without ever running a protractor. That's like detailing a car you never aligned. Start with the fundamentals. Master alignment, and you'll hear your records the way they were meant to sound.
